The water inside the zinc bucket was murky, smelling of deep-shelf kelp, crushed cockle shells, and the raw, cold salt of a moon tide.

Umika Minagi took another step across the damp floor of Preservation Lab 3. Her pale, bare feet made soft, wet slapping sounds against the concrete, leaving faint prints of sea-mist in the dust. Her loose white sailor blouse swayed, unbuttoned at the top two eyelets, and the oversized fisherman's windbreaker hung off her slender shoulders like a shed pupal skin.

"Kaidō-kun," Chihaya whispered. The pen in her hand trembled with enough force that ink spat onto the edge of her pristine abstract paper. "Who... what... why is there a barefoot urchin standing in my certified bio-hazard facility carrying industrial wastewater?!"

"She isn't an urchin," I said, rising from my stool. "Urchins belong to the class *Echinoidea*, characterized by radial symmetry and calcareous test spines. This is a human female. Specifically, the girl from Moon-Crescent Bay."

"I don't care about her taxonomic classification!" Chihaya snapped, slamming both hands onto the stainless-steel island. "This laboratory contains three hundred liters of high-grade formaldehyde, rare abyssal samples from the Ryūgū trench, and an electron microscope subsidized by the Kujirazaki Oceanarium Foundation! She isn't wearing rubberized safety booties! Her epidermal layer is actively dripping ambient moisture onto my linoleum!"

Umika didn't blink. 

She bypassed Chihaya entirely, sliding past the stainless-steel table with an effortless, serpentine gait that didn't disturb a single glass slide. She halted six inches in front of me. 

Up close, the chill radiating from her skin was unmistakable—a damp, subterranean cold that felt like opening the hatch of an ice-trawler's hold. Her hair had dried slightly in the drafty corridor, shimmering with faint, translucent swirls of silver and turquoise, like the nacre on the interior of an abalone shell.

Slowly, without a word, she raised her left hand.

Her slender, pale index finger reached out and touched my right earlobe.

Her skin was smooth as a water-polished river stone, lacking the microscopic friction of dry epithelial tissue.

"Hey!" Chihaya squawked. "What do you think you're touching?!"

Umika ignored her. Her head tilted slightly to the right, her eyes—iridescent, deep-sea navy flecked with luminous specks like plankton under a lantern—stared into mine with utter focus.

"Warm," she murmured. Her voice had the soft, muffled timbre of sound heard underwater through a diving helmet. "Surface blood is very noisy. Thump-thump. Like a piston."

"Your peripheral capillary temperature is still depressed," I observed, keeping my posture steady as her finger traced the curve of my ear. "Based on dermal contact, your epidermis is hovering at approximately 33.8 degrees Celsius. Walking across bare concrete without rubber indoor slippers drastically increases conductive heat loss through the plantar fascia. Why did you discard your footwear?"

Umika looked down at her bare feet, wiggling her toes against the cold floor.

"Slippers are bad," she said flatly. "They pinch like clam valves. The stone wants to speak with the skin. Why do surface humans wrap leather around their fins?"

"Those are feet, not fins," I corrected. "And leather provides traction against crushed barnacle shells and diesel slicks. Furthermore, what is in the bucket?"

At the mention of the bucket, Umika’s shoulders lowered by a fraction of an inch. She set the battered zinc pail onto the stainless-steel dissection table, right next to Chihaya's mass-spectrometry monitor.

*CLANG.*

"Do not place harbor refuse on my workstation!" Chihaya gasped, lunging forward with a bottle of laboratory disinfectant.

"Wait, Senpai," I said, holding up a hand. "Look at the surface tension."

Chihaya paused, her spray nozzle hovering mid-air.

Inside the zinc pail, swirling through three liters of dark seawater, was a creature the size of a tea saucer. At first glance, it resembled a common hermit crab tucked inside a cracked turban shell. But as it shifted against the metal bottom, its legs emerged—long, translucent, and jointed with delicate, glass-like carapaces that glowed with a rhythmic, pulsating sapphire luminescence.

Chihaya’s jaw dropped. Her red-framed glasses slid down her nose. "That's... that's a *Bathynomus* symbiont? No, look at the antennae structure. That's an abyssal ghost-hermit! They only inhabit depths below eight hundred meters along the volcanic vents of the Ryūgū trench! How could a live specimen survive in an unpressurized bucket?!"

"I found him by the tide-glass rocks," Umika said quietly, peering into the water. "He was lonely. The tide went down, but he forgot how to swim backward. So I brought him."

"You... you 'brought him'?!" Chihaya looked ready to tear her hair out. "You carried an uncataloged abyssal decapod through the school corridors like a stray goldfish?!"

"The water has elevated mineral salinity," I noted, dipping a pinky into the bucket and tasting it. "It's near-shore benthic upwelling water. The deep cold currents are rising toward the coast."

Before Chihaya could resume her cross-examination, the heavy wooden door of the lab swung open with a sharp, measured knock followed by a firm push.

"Minagi-san!"

Standing in the doorway, breathing with controlled cadence and holding an official leather-bound folio, was the Student Council President.

Shiori Ryūgū had retied her hair combs and straightened her blazer, though a hint of residual color still lingered on the high points of her cheekbones. She held an emergency brass gate key in one hand and the school registry folio in the other.

"There you are," Shiori said, stepping into the lab with crisp, authoritative strides. "I turned my back for three minutes to finalize your ledger with the dean's office, and you vanish into the basement wing. You were instructed to remain in the administrative annex."

"President," I nodded. "I see the ginger root took effect. Your pulse rate at the carotid artery appears down to roughly seventy-two beats per minute."

Shiori stiffened, her jaw tightening as she pointedly averted her eyes from mine.

"That is completely irrelevant, Kaidō-san," she said coldly. "And I did not ingest your dock-scraps. I merely held a sliver under my tongue for sensory evaluation. Minagi-san, your enrollment documentation is processed. As your legal municipal sponsor and designated shrine guardian, I have completed your preliminary matriculation. You are assigned to this facility."

Chihaya folded her arms, stepping forward with narrowed eyes behind her glasses.

"Hold on just a moment, Ryūgū-san," Chihaya said, her voice dripping with sharp, academic skepticism. "By what authority did you assign an unregistered ward to the Marine Preservation Laboratory? This is a specialized research facility funded by the Kujirazaki Foundation, not a municipal holding pen."

Shiori turned, meeting Chihaya’s glare without flinching.

"By the authority of the Kaiyō High School Club Charter, Section 8," Shiori replied smoothly, opening the folio. "As of two o'clock this afternoon, Umika Minagi is officially enrolled as a special-admissions transfer student under the cultural custody of the Ryūgū Shrine. Furthermore, your club had precisely two members: you and Kaidō-san. Under Article 12 of the prefectural extracurricular budget code, any organization with fewer than three active members faces immediate dissolution and total reclamation of its equipment by the Student Council."

Chihaya froze. The color drained from her cheeks. "You wouldn't dare."

"I would," Shiori replied evenly. "In fact, the dissolution notice was sitting on my blotter this morning. However, the shrine requires Minagi-san to remain in an environment equipped with continuous raw-seawater flumes and professional biological monitoring. You need a third member to maintain your club budget. We need a secure, seawater-connected room under the supervision of someone who understands marine vitals. You are now a legally compliant three-member club. You may thank me by keeping her within this room."

"I don't want shrine charity!" Chihaya fumed. "This is an empirical taxonomic institute, not a temple annex!"

"She is a *Yoriko*," Shiori countered, her voice dropping into a solemn, guarded register. "The shrine bells have been ringing unprovoked at high tide since the spring equinox. Three days ago, deep-sea sulfur sediment washed up on the sacred rocks of the inner torii. Whatever brought her to Moon-Crescent Bay is not an isolated event, Kujirazaki-san. The shrine elders are already preparing purification offerings for a trench disturbance."

"Purification offerings?" Chihaya scoffed, adjusting her glasses with disdain. "Dumping rice wine into the ocean won't alter bathymetric pressures or geothermal venting! If there's an upwelling in the Ryūgū trench, it's caused by tectonic subduction or thermal convection, not angry sea kami!"

While the two of them argued over the boundary between Shinto folklore and oceanographic data, Umika leaned closer to the observation window at the rear of the laboratory.

The back wall of Preservation Lab 3 was not concrete; it was an eight-inch-thick wall of reinforced marine acrylic that looked directly into Kaiyō High School's subterranean seawater flume—a massive, sixty-foot trench carved into the cliff base that drew raw, unfiltered water from Shiozaki Bay to supply the school's aquaculture tanks.

Usually, the flume was a quiet, shadowy turquoise, occasionally crossed by stray surfperch or juvenile rockfish drifting in with the tide.

*Click.*

Inside the zinc bucket on the steel table, the abyssal ghost-hermit crab began scrabbling frantically against the metal walls. Its blue bioluminescent legs flickered, shifting rapidly from sapphire to a warning flash of harsh, jagged violet.

Umika went completely rigid.

She pressed both pale palms flat against the cold acrylic of the observation wall.

"Tsubasa," she whispered. Her voice carried a low, harmonic vibration that hummed through the glass. "The cold water is bleeding."

I stepped around the dissection table, my boots clicking against the concrete, and stood beside her at the glass.

The seawater inside the flume wasn't dark turquoise anymore.

Rising from the deep intake gate forty feet below, a heavy, phosphorescent crimson plume was billowing into the channel. It wasn't silt or industrial runoff; it was a luminous, rust-red bloom that glowed under the dim fluorescent tubes, carrying thick ribbons of gelatinous benthic mucus.

"What is that?" Shiori asked, her argument with Chihaya cut short as the red glow illuminated the basement walls. Her hand drifted to the sea-glass pendant at her neck, her breathing suddenly rapid. "The tide-well... it's the same color as the water in the shrine's sacred spring this afternoon."

"A toxic dinoflagellate bloom?" Chihaya muttered, rushing to the water intake monitor on the wall. "No... look at the sensor readouts. The salinity is 35.8 parts per thousand, and the temperature just dropped six degrees in forty seconds. That's not surface algae. That's an abyssal upwelling!"

"It's a benthic purge," I said, watching the crimson plume swirl against the acrylic. "A deep geothermal rupture along the trench wall is venting hydrogen sulfide and boiling mineral salts into the lower thermocline. It's stripping the dissolved oxygen out of the bottom water. Everything living below five hundred meters is being suffocated and forced upward into the shallow currents."

"Then the fishermen..." Shiori whispered, staring at the glowing water. "The dawn fleet sets their drift nets along the shelf tonight. If the toxic vent reaches the coastal banks—"

"The skipjack and pelagic mackerel will die in the nets before sunrise," I said. "And the sky-whales will refuse to surface through an anoxic vapor layer. The entire harbor fishery will choke."

Before Chihaya could reach for the emergency intake shut-off valve, a massive shadow crested over the flume's intake sill.

It moved with slow, exhausted undulations—a long, ribbon-like body over seven meters in length, its flanks shimmering with mirror-bright silver scales that caught the eerie crimson light. Crowned above its blunt, prehistoric head was an immense, fan-like dorsal crest of fiery scarlet rays, trailing through the water like tattered silk banners.

Its large, golden eyes were dilated, clouded with milky fluid, and its gill covers flapped in rapid, desperate gasps.

"Ryūgū no Tsukai," I said quietly. *Regalecus glesne.* A giant oarfish.

"A live specimen..." Chihaya breathed, her face inches from the acrylic, her scientific arrogance completely consumed by shock. "In less than four fathoms of water? That's impossible. Their swim bladders and gelatinous tissue can't tolerate surface decompression!"

"It didn't come here to deliver a prophecy," I said, pointing to the erratic, spasming flutter of the creature's pectoral fins. "Look at the gill flutter and the nitrogen bubbles forming along the lateral line. It's suffering from acute decompression sickness. It fled the sulfide vent at the bottom of the trench, shot up through the water column too fast, and got swept into the academy's intake gate by the rising moon tide."

The oarfish drifted closer, its silver flank scraping against the acrylic with a dull, hollow scrape. It rolled onto its side, struggling to right itself against the flume's intake current.

"If it stays in this shallow flume, the pressure drop and heat will rupture its internal organs in less than twenty minutes," I continued, glancing at the water intake console. "And if that crimson sulfide plume breaches the school's aquaculture holding pens, every specimen in this building will be dead by morning."

Umika pressed her forehead against the vibrating acrylic, her eyes reflecting the dying creature's silver scales.

"He's choking," she murmured. Her fingers traced the glass where the oarfish's crimson crest swept past. "The mountain at the bottom of the sea is coughing fire. If the gate stays open, the fire will fill the bay."

Shiori looked from the dying leviathan to the crimson water, her knuckles white around her clipboard. "The shrine elders were right about the disturbance, but wrong about the cause. This isn't an omen to be appeased with salt and sake. It's happening right now."

"We can't just close the intake valve from this console," Chihaya said, tapping the emergency panel. A red warning light flashed on the board: *VALVE FAILURE - ACTUATOR OVERLOADED.* "The benthic silt has jammed the hydraulic gate at the cliff base! The primary bypass has to be manually cranked from the lower tide-lock on the harbor pier!"

I grabbed my heavy canvas deck-coat off the wall hook and checked the manual pressure gauge on the side of my bag.

"The tide-lock on the southern pier uses a five-ton worm gear," I said. "The Fishermen's Union won't touch it without an emergency harbor order, and the city council won't convene until eight o'clock tomorrow morning."

"I hold the hereditary seal of the Ryūgū maritime precinct," Shiori said, stepping forward, her voice hardening with sudden, resolute authority. "The union docks answer to the shrine's tide charter. If I order the gate bypassed, the dockmaster cannot refuse."

"And I have the hydrographic acoustic telemetry from the Kujirazaki buoy array," Chihaya added, ripping the printed bathymetric chart off the wall clipboard. "I can track the plume's velocity across the bay."

"Then we have approximately fourteen minutes before the anoxic plume hits the harbor nursery," I said, pulling my rubber harbor gloves from my coat pocket. "And before this oarfish's swim bladder turns inside out. Minagi-san, put on shoes."

Umika looked down at the pair of heavy canvas deck-sneakers I pulled from under the specimen bench, then back up at me. A faint, knowing curve touched the corner of her lips.

"The sea is loud," she said, slipping her feet into the oversized shoes without tying the laces. "Let's go close the gate."
