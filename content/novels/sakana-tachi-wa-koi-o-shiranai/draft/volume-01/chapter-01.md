The final three steps of the Great Otokozaka were slick with whale-mist and smelled intensely of ozone.

Matsuri hit the gravel plateau before the school gate with a desperate slide, her rubber-soled school loafers throwing up a rooster-tail of wet crushed granite. I cleared the threshold half a pace behind her, one hand clamping my school bag shut to prevent the yellowtail collar bento from undergoing catastrophic lateral displacement.

*Chiiiiiiime.*

The high-brass chime of Kaiyō High School’s first bell echoed across the bluffs, vibrating the morning dew off the cedar windbreaks.

"Safe!" Matsuri gasped, planting her palms on her knees. Her chest heaved against her navy blazer, steam rising from her damp ponytail in the brisk morning air. "We're safe! Tell me the proctor didn't see us!"

"Your definition of 'safe' neglects basic optics," I said, catching my breath and checking my barometer. "We crossed the threshold with four seconds of margin, which technically classifies us as punctual under Section 4, Paragraph B of the municipal attendance charter. However, our presence has already been visually acquired."

Matsuri stiffened. Slowly, like a rusted winch turning against three tons of kelp, she raised her head.

Standing beneath the shadow of the heavy wooden Torii gate that served as Kaiyō High School’s western entrance was a girl holding an aluminum clipboard with both hands.

She wore the standard Kaiyō blazer, but hers was impeccably pressed, devoid of the salt-stains and diesel streaks that marked anyone living south of the cliff road. Her obsidian hair fell in straight, silk-smooth sheets past her shoulders, pinned behind her ears with lacquered tortoiseshell combs bearing the three-wave crest of the Ryūgū Shrine. A gold arm-band pinned to her left sleeve read *Student Council President* in immaculate brush calligraphy.

Shiori Ryūgū tapped a mechanical pencil against the aluminum clip. *Click. Click. Click.*

"Four seconds before homeroom bell," Shiori said. Her voice was like crushed sea-glass—crisp, pristine, and perfectly measured. "Hardly a margin befitting second-year students of a prefectural maritime academy, Kaidō-san. Isaki-san."

"Ryūgū-senpai!" Matsuri instantly straightened up, tucking her hands behind her back, though her jaw remained obstinately jutted out. "The celestial siren went off! The Great Otokozaka was caught directly under the downdraft wake of an *Amanokujira*! You can't issue demerits for acts of god!"

"The migration schedule of the pod was posted on the harbor bulletin board forty-eight hours ago," Shiori replied without looking up from her roster. "A disciplined student anticipates aerial barometric shifts. A negligent student uses magnificent ocean fauna as an excuse for uncombed hair and poorly tied neckties."

Her dark eyes finally lifted from the paper, sweeping over Matsuri's wind-tousled bangs before landing squarely on me.

Her nose twitched. Just a millimeter.

"Kaidō-san."

"Good morning, Student Council President," I said respectfully.

"You smell of raw skipjack blood, WD-40, and brine," she declared, her upper lip curling slightly in distaste. "Did you sleep in an engine bilge again?"

"I was assisting the third-shift trawlers on the southern pier between four-thirty and six AM," I explained. "The dawn skipjack had elevated lactic acid levels due to an offshore thermocline collapse; rapid bleeding was essential to prevent enzymatic degradation of the flesh. As for the WD-40, the coastal salt air causes galvanic corrosion in the zinc winch gears if they aren't sprayed post-rinse."

"This is an educational institution, not a fish-gutting cooperative," Shiori said coldly. She stepped forward, her posture rigid, her chin tilted at the precise fifteen-degree angle prescribed for shrine maidens greeting imperial envoys. "If you bring harbor refuse into the second-year corridor, I will personally see to it that the Disciplinary Committee assigns you to barnacle scraping on the tide-intake flumes for the next two—"

She stopped.

A micro-tremor ran through her knuckles. Her knuckles whitened against the aluminum clipboard. The color drained from her lips, leaving them the pale, chalky gray of bleached coral.

I stepped forward and leaned in slightly, squinting at her face.

"K-Kaidō-san?!" Shiori gasped, recoiling half an inch. Her eyes widened, a sudden rush of crimson breaking through her pale cheeks. "What are you doing?! Maintain standard personal distance!"

"Pupil dilation is asymmetrical by approximately seven percent," I observed clinically. "Your cervical vertebrae are locked in an involuntary compensatory stiffening, and your breathing pattern indicates shallow thoracic compensation. You're suffering from vestibular canal disturbance."

"I am doing no such thing! Step back!"

"It was the infrasound from the humpback's breach," I continued, unperturbed. "Celestial Humpbacks emit a twenty-hertz subterranean hum during atmospheric decompression. When that frequency bounces off the basalt bluffs, people with sensitive inner-ear fluid balance experience acute vertical vertigo. In short: you are seasick on dry land."

"I am *not* seasick!" Shiori’s voice cracked into a sharp, mortified pitch. She took a frantic step back, but her balance wobbled on the gravel; her right ankle gave way beneath her.

Before her knees could hit the dirt, I caught her elbow, stabilizing her center of gravity against the stone post of the gate.

Her body felt shockingly light, vibrating like a plucked fluorocarbon guitar string. Close up, she smelled not of fish or sea-salt, but of peppermint pastilles and pressed temple incense. 

"Let go of me!" she hissed, though she didn't have the strength to pull away. Her face was now burning a furious, humiliated scarlet that rivaled a boiled spiny lobster. "I am the daughter of the Ryūgū Shrine! We have commanded the tides of Shiozaki for seven hundred years! The sea does not make me ill!"

"The human inner ear cares very little for ancestral pedigree," I noted reasonably. I reached into my coat pocket and retrieved a small, wax-paper packet. "Here. Peppermint won't solve it; it only masks nausea. This is dried ginger root crystallized in coarse sea-salt and roasted kelp powder. Chew half a slice. The gingerols inhibit serotonin receptors in the vestibular nuclei, and the sodium stabilizes your blood pressure."

Shiori stared at the grimy wax packet in my hand as if I had offered her a live blowfish.

"You... you expect me to put dock-scraps into my mouth?!"

"Suit yourself. But if you collapse during the morning assembly, the third-year vice president will have to read the tide warnings, and his pronunciation of Latin pelagic classifications is atrocious."

Matsuri, who had been watching this interaction with her arms crossed and her foot tapping aggressively against the gravel, suddenly grabbed my sleeve and yanked me backward with the force of an offshore gale.

"Hey! Don't go shoving weird fisherman remedies into the Council President's face, Wing-head!" Matsuri barked, glaring between the two of us. Her cheeks were oddly flushed again, her teeth clamped tight. "Come on! The warning bell is going to ring any second, and I am not getting detention because you decided to play coastal witch doctor at the front gate!"

"Take the packet, President," I said, setting it neatly on the corner of her wooden inspection lectern. "And drink warm barley tea, not cold water."

Matsuri dragged me by my blazer collar down the stone flagstones toward the main building. 

Behind us, through the morning fog, I looked back for a fraction of a second. 

Shiori was still standing beneath the Torii gate, completely alone. She looked at the wax packet on the podium. Slowly, furtively, looking left and right to confirm that no other disciplinary officers were in sight, her slender fingers reached out, plucked the dried ginger from the paper, and slipped it into her mouth.

***

Second period was Celestial Navigation and Atmospheric Meteorology, held in Classroom 2-B on the second floor of the seaside wing.

Outside the open sash windows, the sky had returned to its deceptive, crystal-clear blue. But fifty meters below the cliff edge, the Pacific swell was crashing with rhythmic, booming violence, sending plumes of white foam shooting up the black rocks like fireworks.

"...and as we observe during vernal equinox conditions," droned Mr. Kameyama, our homeroom proctor, tapping a dusty wooden pointer against a blackboard diagram of high-altitude isobar lines, "the convergence of the Kuroshio ocean current and the upper troposphere jet stream creates a localized pocket of zero-shear vapor lift. This is why our town enjoys a Grade Three or higher aerial fishery throughout April."

*Thud.*

My forehead met the smooth pine surface of my desk.

It wasn't that the lecture was uninteresting; it was simply that the thermal mechanics of cloud-lift had been drummed into my bones since I was six years old. When you spend three hours every morning hauling thirty-kilo skipjack out of brine slush at near-freezing temperatures, the monotone cadence of a fifty-year-old teacher talking about barometric pressure operates on the brain with the sedative potency of chloroform.

*Snap.*

A small rubber eraser bounced off my ear.

I lifted an eyelid. Across the narrow aisle, Matsuri was glaring at me over the top of her open navigation chart. She made a sharp cutting motion across her throat with her forefinger, then pointed violently toward the front of the room.

"Kaidō-kun," Mr. Kameyama's voice suddenly cut through the hum of the ceiling fans.

I lifted my head and sat up straight. "Yes, Sensei."

"Since you find the surface of your desk so deeply engaging, perhaps you can enlighten the class," Kameyama said, adjusting his wire-rimmed spectacles with a smirk that promised public execution. "Suppose an *Amanokujira* traveling northbound on the Tenkai-ryū encounters a sudden cold front over Cape Shiozaki at 800 hectopascals. What will be its immediate hydrodynamic response, and how should coastal traffic adjust?"

The classroom went dead quiet. Several boys in the back row winced in sympathetic pity. Matsuri buried her face in her hands, groaning softly.

"The question contains an erroneous premise," I replied smoothly.

Kameyama paused, his pointer freezing against the blackboard. "Excuse me?"

"A cold front at 800 hectopascals does not produce a uniform thermal drop in the Shiozaki airspace," I explained, leaning back slightly. "Because of the subterranean geothermal vents under the bay, the sea-surface temperature remains constant at 14.2 degrees Celsius. When the cold front hits, it creates a wedge of negative buoyancy at the mid-cloud tier, forcing the leviathan to dive forty fathoms into the maritime boundary layer to seek warm thermals. It will not maintain level flight. It will drop rapidly toward the sea surface at an angle of roughly twelve degrees."

I pointed out the classroom window toward the southern headland.

"Therefore, commercial skiffs should immediately clear the littoral shallows, whereas high-altitude sky-nets should be reeled in by sixty percent to prevent cable snapping. If you maintain standard cruising formation, the whale’s tail turbulence will capsize every wooden vessel within two nautical miles."

Silence reigned in Classroom 2-B.

Mr. Kameyama stared at me. He looked at his lesson plan binder. He turned a page, looked at his notes, and turned back.

"...That is... technically correct," he muttered, clearing his throat and hastily scribbling a formula on the board. "Though your textbook's simplified model assumes an idealized laminar flow. Very well. Kaidō-kun, stay awake."

"Yes, Sensei."

Matsuri let out a long, audible sigh across the aisle, collapsing against her desk like a punctured life raft. She mouthed three words across the gap:

*Show-off mackerel brain.*

I gave her a polite nod of acknowledgment and went back to checking the barometric drift on my watch.

***

When the lunch bell rang at 12:40, the classroom erupted into the chaotic scrape of chair legs and the rustle of plastic wrappers.

Matsuri stood up instantly, grabbed her chair by the backrest, slammed it down directly in front of my desk with enough force to rattle my pencil case, and sat down backwards, crossing her arms over the wooden frame.

She didn't say anything. She just stared at me.

Her hazel eyes were narrowed like a reef shark assessing a wounded seal. Her brows were furrowed into a sharp, expectant V, her chestnut ponytail swaying with every impatient tap of her foot.

"Yes, Matsuri?" I asked, unzipping my bag.

"The box," she commanded.

"The bento box?"

"What else, you idiot?! Are you going to eat it, or are you going to stare at it until it turns into a museum specimen?!"

"Proper digestion requires an unhurried, parasympathetic state," I noted, carefully placing the indigo-wrapped cedar box onto my desk. 

With meticulous care, I untied the fisherman's hitch. I folded the indigo cloth into neat, crisp quarters, laying it aside like surgical linen, before lifting the lid.

The fragrance of aged soy sauce, sweet mirin, and braised ginger bloomed into the air, drawing a sharp, appreciative sniff from two boys sitting three desks over. The yellowtail collar sat nestled against a bed of steamed white rice, flanked by pickled daikon and tender burdock root. The glaze had settled into a deep, glossy amber.

I picked up my cedar chopsticks, separated them with a clean snap, and bowed my head. "I receive this sustenance."

Matsuri didn't breathe. Her hands clenched tightly around the back of the chair, her knuckles turning bone-white.

I inserted the tips of the chopsticks into the thickest part of the cheek meat. The flesh yielded with zero resistance, parting along the natural muscle fibers like butter. A glistening bead of rendered fat pooled along the silver skin. 

I placed the morsel in my mouth and closed my eyes.

For five seconds, I did not speak. I masticated with deliberate, analytical precision, letting the flavor profile break down across the taste buds of my tongue.

"Well?!" Matsuri exploded, unable to endure the silence for another second. She leaned so far across the back of the chair that her nose was barely six inches from mine. "Is it salty?! Is the ginger too strong?! Did the skin get soggy from the steam?! My mom said fifteen minutes of simmering was plenty, but I kept it on low heat for twenty-five because the collar cut was unusually thick, and if the fat didn't render properly it would taste fishy, and—"

"The thermal denaturation of the collagen is sublime," I announced, opening my eyes.

Matsuri choked mid-sentence. "The... the what?"

"Yellowtail collar is rich in Type-I collagen fibers and high-density polyunsaturated fatty acids," I explained, gesturing at the delicate bone structure with the tip of my chopstick. "If simmered too rapidly at high temperatures, the muscle bundles seize and expel their intracellular moisture, resulting in a fibrous, chalky texture. However, by holding the simmer at approximately eighty-five degrees for twenty-five minutes, you allowed the gelatinization process to complete without scorching the outer epidermis."

I took a bite of the rice, perfectly coated in the braising liquor.

"Furthermore, the ratio of ginger to mirin has completely neutralized the trimethylamine oxide compounds without overpowering the intrinsic umami of the pelagic fat. Matsuri, this preparation demonstrates an intuitive mastery of marine biochemistry that rivals a three-star ryokan chef."

Matsuri sat frozen.

Slowly, inexorably, the heat began to rise. It started at her collarbones, swept up the slender column of her throat, and engulfed her cheeks, ears, and temples in a thermonuclear crimson that seemed almost capable of igniting paper.

"Y-You..." she stammered, her mouth opening and closing soundlessly like a beached carp. "You... you absolute... moron!"

"Did I misidentify the cooking duration?" I asked, taking another bite of the tender cheek meat. "The texture suggests twenty-five minutes, but if you used an induction simmer plate—"

"I asked if it tasted good, you hopeless fish-nerd! Not for a doctoral defense on fat molecules!" 

She leaped to her feet, knocking her chair backward against the linoleum with a deafening clatter. The entire classroom went quiet, half a dozen students turning to watch the spectacle with amused, knowing grins.

"Eat it all! Every single grain of rice!" she yelled, pointing an accusatory, trembling finger at my face. "If you leave behind even one piece of ginger, I will throw you off the lighthouse pier after school!"

She spun on her heel, her skirt flaring out, and stormed out of the classroom into the hallway, slamming the sliding door behind her with enough force to shake the glass panes.

"A truly volatile temperament," I remarked to myself, picking up another succulent morsel of fish. "High blood pressure runs in coastal families. I should recommend more potassium in her diet."

***

The final bell of the day rang at 3:30 PM. 

While the majority of Kaiyō High School's student body poured out toward the sports fields, the sky-skiff hangars, or the commercial shopping arcade down by the harbor, I turned away from the sunlight and headed down the central stone staircase toward the old wing.

Down, past the gymnasium lockers. Down, past the boiler rooms where the salt-air exhaust hummed day and night.

At the very end of a cold, damp subterranean corridor lined with exposed seawater pipes and flickering fluorescent tubes hung a frosted-glass door bearing a tarnished brass plaque:

**MARINE SPECIMEN & ECOLOGY CLUB**
*(Preservation Lab 3)*

I pushed the door open.

The smell struck me immediately—that comforting, familiar blend of formaldehyde, 90% ethanol, sea-moss, and the faint, sweet metallic tang of dried kelp. 

The room was vast, high-ceilinged, and crowded with towering steel shelves reaching up into the shadows. Upon those shelves sat hundreds of glass specimen jars of every imaginable size. Some contained pale, ghostly deep-sea isopods curled into armored balls; others held the luminous, sapphire-tipped tentacles of aerial siphonophores suspended in alcohol. At the far end of the room, hanging from the reinforced concrete ceiling by heavy steel chains, was the articulated, three-meter skeleton of an infant cloud-porpoise, its ribs gleaming like polished ivory in the dim light.

Sitting at the central stainless-steel dissection island was a girl wearing a spotless white laboratory coat over her school uniform.

Her crimson-framed glasses reflected the blue glow of an electron microscope monitor. Her sleek black hair was tied back in a severe, no-nonsense chignon secured by a titanium dissection probe. Spread across the steel counter before her were dozens of petri dishes, a precision micro-centrifuge, and a stack of printed statistical charts that looked thick enough to stop a harpoon.

Chihaya Kujirazaki did not look up from her microscope.

"You're seven minutes late, Kaidō-kun," she announced, her voice clipped, haughty, and dripping with aristocratic disdain. "A proper research assistant does not linger in the hallways when empirical science is waiting."

"I am a registered member of this club, Kujirazaki-senpai, not your indentured laboratory assistant," I replied, setting my school bag on the leather sofa in the corner. "Furthermore, I was delayed because Matsuri insisted on reclaiming her empty bento box with three separate verbal threats."

At the mention of Matsuri, Chihaya’s pen halted on her clipboard. She turned her head, adjusting her red glasses with a slender, pale middle finger.

"That harbor barbarian," Chihaya scoffed, curling her lips into a superior smirk. "Wasting high-grade pelagic protein on someone whose palate is calibrated solely for dockside rations. Regardless, your domestic squabbles are of zero consequence. Look at the primary monitor."

I walked over to the stainless-steel table and leaned over her shoulder to inspect the screen.

Displayed on the monitor was a high-resolution microscopic feed of a liquid droplet. Suspended within the fluid were thousands of tiny, crystalline structures—hexagonal prisms that glowed with a faint, iridescent violet phosphorescence.

"Atmospheric condensation collected from the southern meteorological tower during the morning breach," Chihaya said, her voice rising with unmistakable pride. She tapped the glass screen with the tip of her titanium probe. "Using my family foundation’s proprietary mass-spectrometry array, I have spent the last three hours analyzing the vapor plume of that *Amanokujira*. Observe the lipid crystalline lattice. It is completely unprecedented."

She spun her swivel chair around, crossing one black-stockinged leg over the other, and looked up at me with a triumphant glare.

"I have already drafted the preliminary abstract for the National Oceanographic Academy. I hypothesize that the celestial humpback utilizes an endogenous super-cooled biological surfactant to reduce atmospheric drag across its epidermal layer! A groundbreaking discovery that will cement Kaiyō High School’s academic funding for the next three fiscal years. Well? What do you say, Kaidō-kun? Are you prepared to concede the superiority of rigorous academic methodology over primitive dockside superstition?"

I didn't answer immediately. 

I picked up the micropipette from the rack, drew a half-microliter drop of the fluid from the open petri dish, and deposited it onto the back of my left hand. 

"Hey! What are you doing?!" Chihaya gasped, her composure instantly shattering as she jumped out of her chair. "That is an unsterilized biological sample from an eighty-ton celestial megafauna! You cannot simply smear it on your skin like hand lotion! The contamination risk—"

I brought my hand to my nose and inhaled deeply. Then, before Chihaya could physically tackle me, I touched the tip of my tongue to the residue.

"K-Kaidō!" she shrieked, her face turning bright red behind her glasses. "Are you insane?! Spitting in the face of laboratory safety protocols—!"

"It's not surfactant," I said calmly.

Chihaya froze mid-shriek. "What?"

"The lipid crystallization isn't an epidermal drag-reduction agent," I said, wiping my hand with a clean paper towel. "The taste is bitter, with a high concentration of sulfurous esters and broken chitin enzymes. And the hexagonal lattice you're seeing isn't a synthetic lipid matrix. It's digested aerial krill bile."

Chihaya stared at me, her mouth hanging slightly open. "B-Bile...?"

"The humpback you sampled wasn't cruising," I explained, leaning over the counter and pointing to a small cluster of broken cellular debris on the monitor. "Look at the fragmentation in the chitin walls. Those are the carapaces of *Sergestes serpens*—cloud-krill. The whale had just finished feeding on an aerial swarm over the outer banks twenty minutes before the breach. When it crested through the cold front, the sudden barometric drop caused a mild gastric reflux. What you have collected and analyzed with your multi-million-yen mass spectrometer, Senpai, is approximately fifty milliliters of celestial whale vomit."

The silence in Preservation Lab 3 was so total that you could hear the slow, rhythmic dripping of the seawater intake valves three rooms away.

Chihaya stood completely paralyzed. 

Her red-rimmed glasses slipped two millimeters down the bridge of her nose. Her gaze drifted slowly from my face, down to the petri dish on the stainless-steel table, then to the stack of fifty printed abstract pages titled *Novel Epidermal Surfactants of Celestial Cetaceans: A Quantitative Study*.

A tiny, high-pitched squeak escaped her throat.

"V-Vomit...?" she whispered, trembling from the tips of her patent-leather shoes to the crown of her head. "I... I spent my entire afternoon... analyzing... cetacean regurgitation?!"

"It's extraordinarily high quality regurgitation," I offered helpfully, trying to soften the blow. "The preservation of the krill enzymes is exceptional. If you dry it and combine it with pine resin, it makes an unbeatable waterproofing wax for coastal skiff hulls."

"Shut up!" 

Chihaya grabbed a leather-bound volume of *The International Taxonomic Index of Pelagic Fauna* and slammed it down onto the metal table with a thunderous *CRACK*.

"Shut up, shut up, shut up! Why do you always do this?!" She leaned across the table, her glasses fogging slightly with pure indignation, her face mere inches from mine. "I am the top-ranked student in the prefecture! I have published two peer-reviewed papers on celestial benthic ecology! And you—a dock-rat who sleeps through civics and smells like salt-mackerel—you identify an unknown biochemical compound by *tasting* it?!"

"Taste and smell are the most primitive, reliable sensor arrays available to the biological organism," I said mildly. "Your computer models only see numbers, Senpai. You have to understand the animal."

"I understand that you are the most insufferable, exasperating, infuriating creature to ever wash up on the shores of this prefecture!" she fumed. But even as she glared at me, her eyes darted to the monitor, taking in the chitin fragmentation I had pointed out. A reluctant, burning curiosity flickered behind her frustration. 

She bit her lower lip, turning her face away with a haughty sniff.

"...Sit down, Kaidō-kun."

"I should check the filter on the deep-sea tank—"

"I said sit down!" She pulled an empty stool over and shoved it behind my knees with her foot. "Since your vulgar palate is apparently more calibrated than my spectrometer, you are going to assist me in re-indexing the remaining forty samples before sunset. And if you dare speak a single word of this 'gastric reflux' incident to that insufferable Student Council President, I will personally dissolve your locker in hydrochloric acid."

"Understood," I said, taking a seat. "Though hydrochloric acid reacts violently with the galvanized steel of the school lockers. You would be better served using concentrated nitric acid."

Chihaya let out a sound that was half-sob, half-growl, and buried her forehead into her hands.

Before I could uncap the next sample vial, the heavy wooden door of the lab swung open with a quiet, unoiled creak.

Both of us looked up.

Standing in the doorway, framed against the amber light of the subterranean corridor, was a girl clutching a brand-new navy-blue school bag against her chest.

She was wearing the Kaiyō High School uniform, but clearly had no idea how it was supposed to function. The white sailor blouse was untucked, the blue neckerchief hung loose and unknotted around her collarbones, and over her shoulders she had draped an oversized, salt-bleached fisherman's windbreaker. Most glaringly of all, her regulation indoor slippers were nowhere in sight; her bare feet rested pale and cold against the concrete floor, leaving small, damp outlines of moisture on the stone.

Her hair was a translucent, ethereal shade of silver-blue, tumbling down her back like the spray of a breaking wave under moonlight.

In her right hand, dangling by a twisted hemp cord, she carried a battered zinc bucket sloshing with dark seawater.

"Excuse me," Chihaya began, straightening up with an immediate scowl as her laboratory decorum took over. "This is the Marine Preservation Laboratory. General students are not permitted without safety goggles, and walking barefoot through a chemical specimen facility is a grotesque violation of prefectural hygiene codes—"

The girl didn't look at Chihaya. Her wide, sea-glass eyes drifted slowly past the glowing specimen jars, past the cloud-porpoise skeleton, and settled squarely on me.

My breath caught in my throat.

*Three weeks ago.*

It had been four in the morning during a bioluminescent spring tide out at secluded Moon-Crescent Bay. I had gone out to the tide pools testing the acoustic frequency of my carved conch shell, calling out to a wandering pod of infant celestial whales off the reef, when the receding surf had deposited a bundle of tangled oarweed onto the black sands. 

Inside the kelp ribbons had been this very girl—barely breathing, her skin cold as benthic ice. 

I had checked her vitals, wrapped her in my spare dry burlap, and rushed her straight to the one place in Shiozaki equipped to deal with unexplained oceanic phenomena: the Ryūgū Shrine. Shiori had answered the shrine gates in her night robes. When Shiori saw the wild, uncanny way the nearby sea-swallows circled the girl's head, the shrine maiden had immediately recognized the sacred signs of a *Yoriko*—a child gifted by the tides. Shiori had promised to shelter her, speak to her family and elders, and handle the paperwork with the academy.

That was three weeks ago. Shiori had kept the details under strict lock and key, refusing to elaborate whenever I asked at the shrine steps.

And now, here she was. In Kaiyō High School.

The girl took one silent, barefoot step across the threshold into the laboratory.

Her gaze fixed on the carved conch shell resting on the corner of my desk beside the barometer, then lifted to my eyes.

"Found you," she whispered. Her voice was soft, devoid of fear or social hesitation, carrying the faint, submerged resonance of an underwater cave. "The boy who talks to the whales."

Beside me, the glass needle of my barometer gave a sharp, violent twitch—and plunged into the deep, unknown blue.
