The smell of hydrogen sulfide hit us halfway down the stone zigzags of the Great Otokozaka.

It wasn't subtle. It cut clean through the fresh salt-mist and diesel fumes of the lower town, carrying that sharp, rotten-egg tang that every commercial diver fears: anoxic bottom water. The subterranean vents along the trench shelf had blown a gasket, and forty tons of boiling sulfurous brine was rising along the continental shelf like grease in a kitchen sink.

Behind me, the sound of labored breathing and mismatched footwear was growing erratic.

"Pick up the stride," I called back over my shoulder, adjusting the heavy canvas strap of my field bag. "At forty-five centimeters of tidal lift per hour, the pressure differential against the lower sluice plate increases by eighty kilograms per minute. If we don't drop the bypass before the tide crests, the gate will seize completely."

"Do not... lecture me on... physical exertion, Kaidō-san!" Shiori panted. 

She was doing her best to maintain her upright, aristocratic posture, but her polished leather school loafers were never engineered for descending two hundred and forty stone steps at a dead sprint. She held the heavy brass seal of the Ryūgū Shrine clenched in her right fist, her uniform neckerchief fluttering wild over her shoulder. Her skin had turned pasty white, her respiration rate locked at a ragged thirty-two breaths a minute.

Right beside her, Chihaya Kujirazaki was in even worse aerodynamic shape. Her white laboratory coat was billowing like a spinnaker in a gale, her red-framed glasses sliding perilously down her sweaty nose with every heavy impact of her patent-leather shoes. In both arms, she clutched a waterproof binder of telemetry printouts like an infant rescued from a fire.

"Kaidō!" Chihaya wheezed, her vocal cords vibrating with unadulterated laboratory rage. "If my... family's five-million-yen... acoustic sonar repeater... gets soaked in salt spray... I will harvest your kidneys... for taxonomic preservation!"

"The acoustic repeater is waterproofed to IP68 standards," I said mildly. "Your current heart rate, however, suggests a severe lack of cardiovascular conditioning. You should consider rowing skiffs instead of staring at mass spectrometers."

"Shut up!"

The only one running without audible distress was Umika. 

She moved in my oversized canvas deck sneakers with a strange, soundless lope—weightless, as if the gravity of the stone steps was an optional recommendation she had chosen to ignore. Her silver-blue hair trailed behind her in ethereal ribbons, and her wide eyes remained fixed on the bay below, where the sapphire water beyond the breakwater was darkening into an ominous, rusted bruise.

We burst through the final Torii arch at the base of the bluff and hit the concrete apron of Pier 4.

The southern harbor at dusk was normally a chaotic, vibrant scene. The day-trawlers were moored four abreast against the wooden pilings, their diesel exhausts chugging blue smoke into the purple twilight while seagulls fought over offal on the cleaning slabs. 

Sitting on an overturned plastic fish crate under the glare of a thousand-watt halogen work light was Matsuri Isaki.

She had stripped off her school blazer, tying the sleeves around her waist. Her white uniform blouse had its sleeves rolled past her elbows, revealing forearms smeared with black winch grease and dried squid ink. Between her knees, she held a heavy spool of 300-pound fluorocarbon line, using a stainless-steel fid to splice a braided steel leader into a tuna longline.

Beside her, two veteran fishermen from the Cooperative Union—old man Genzo with his weathered leather face and missing left incisor, and Kenji, the night dockmaster—were sharing a thermos of roasted barley tea.

Matsuri looked up as our frantic footsteps hammered against the damp concrete.

Her jaw dropped. Her hands froze on the fid.

Her eyes drifted from me, to the Student Council President clutching an ancestral shrine seal, to Chihaya clutching a bundle of satellite telemetry in a flapping lab coat, and finally to Umika, who was wearing my spare deck shoes and an oversized windbreaker with the top buttons undone.

"Wing-head...?" Matsuri said slowly, setting the steel fid down on the crate. Her hazel eyes narrowed into two deadly, assessing slits. "What is this? Did you rob an aquarium, or did you start a traveling circus behind my back?"

"No time," I said, dropping my bag onto the timber deck. "Genzo-san. Kenji-san. We need the bypass lever for the No. 4 lower sluice. Now."

Old man Genzo squinted at me through a cloud of Mild Seven cigarette smoke, then let out a raspy, phlegmatic chuckle. "The lower sluice? Boy, that tide-gate hasn't been cracked since the typhoon three autumns ago. What're you playing at?"

"The flume at Kaiyō High is taking in an anoxic sulfide plume," I said, pointing toward the outer bay where a greasy crimson slick was drifting inward between the breakwater heads. "A hydrothermal vent blew out along the Ryūgū trench shelf forty minutes ago. The dissolved oxygen in the bay is dropping to zero point two milligrams per liter. In twelve minutes, that water hits the nursery cages where the cooperative keeps forty thousand juvenile yellowtail fry."

Kenji, the night dockmaster, snorted and took a sip of his tea. "Sulfide vent? Kid, it's just spring *akashio*—red algae bloom. Happens every couple of years when the weather turns humid. The old guys up at the shrine already called down; they're tossing salt off the cliff to quiet the sea-sprites. Go home and finish your homework."

"This is not a biological dinoflagellate bloom, you stubborn dockhand!" Chihaya snarled, lunging forward and slapping a printout directly across Kenji's tea thermos. 

The dockmaster blinked at the high-resolution thermal imaging map.

"Look at the bathymetric gradient!" Chihaya pointed an ink-stained finger at the red contour spikes. "The acoustic sensors on Buoy Seven recorded a localized seismic heave of 2.1 on the Richter scale, followed by a thermal discharge of 85 degrees Celsius at six hundred meters depth! The plume is carrying free hydrogen sulfide and dissolved silicates! If that enters the shallow nursery, the gills of your juvenile yellowtail will suffer irreversible chemical asphyxiation in less than three hundred seconds!"

Kenji scratched the stubble on his chin, staring at the complex graphs with a blank, illiterate scowl. "Look, missy, fancy school paper don't mean nothing to me. The harbor rules say nobody touches the five-ton floodgates without a written municipal directive from the mayor's office."

"Then look at this," Shiori stepped forward, her voice cutting through the harbor noise like a temple bell.

She withdrew her hand from her coat, holding aloft a polished disk of dark bog-oak inlaid with a three-wave whorl of pure beaten gold. 

Kenji's smirk vanished. 

Old man Genzo actually pulled the cigarette from his mouth, his eyes widening.

"The Sovereign Sea-Charter of the Ryūgū Shrine," Shiori announced, her back ramrod straight, every inch the hereditary maiden. "Under Article 3 of the Shiozaki Maritime Covenant, established in the third year of Genji, the shrine maiden holds absolute jurisdiction over the coastal tide-locks during unhallowed ocean disturbances. Do you dispute the seal of the Dragon Palace, Dockmaster?"

Kenji swallowed hard. His gaze flicked between the golden crest, the glowing red water out in the bay, and Shiori's unyielding eyes.

"N-No, Ryūgū-sama," Kenji stammered, fumbling for the heavy iron ring of keys at his belt. "No dispute. But... but that gate hasn't turned in years! The worm gear is rusted solid, and the lock mechanism takes three men on a six-foot breaker bar just to budge!"

"Matsuri," I said, turning to my childhood friend. "Get the grease gun and the four-foot iron pipe from your father's tool locker."

Matsuri didn't ask another question. The moment she realized the juvenile fry cages were in danger, all personal confusion evaporated, replaced by the grim, ruthless competence of a seasoned harbor hand.

"On it!" she shouted, sprinting toward the gear shed.

She returned five seconds later with a forty-pound hardened steel breaker bar in one hand and a manual pneumatic grease canister in the other.

"The spindle is down on the lower catwalk," Matsuri said, tossing me the grease gun. "Move your asses!"

We vaulted the rusty safety railing and scrambled down the iron ladder to the concrete platform hanging just three feet above the churning, crimson-tinged water. 

The heat radiating off the surface was sickening. It felt like standing over an open sewer grate in midsummer, thick with the stench of brimstone and decaying sea-moss. Down in the water, hundreds of small shore-crabs were crawling frantically up the concrete pilings, desperate to escape the suffocating liquid below.

Mounted on the center of the platform was a massive, cast-iron pedestal housing a five-ton worm gear, coated in layers of salt scale and peeling red primer.

"Spindle is oxidized!" I shouted over the roar of the incoming tide, thrusting the grease nozzle into the intake nipple and pumping the handle with violent, rhythmic thrusts. "Matsuri, slot the bar into the primary ratchet!"

Matsuri slid the heavy steel pipe over the iron drive-shaft, the metal ringing out with a loud *CLANK*.

"Chihaya! Shiori! Back against the bulkhead!" I ordered. "If this shaft snaps under torque, it'll take your legs off!"

"Don't you order me around, Kaidō!" Chihaya yelled, but she quickly retreated to the ladder alongside Shiori, whose eyes were fixed on the dark water churning below the grate.

Matsuri grabbed the end of the breaker bar, planting both rubber deck-boots against the concrete footing. I threw my weight in beside her, our shoulders locking tight as we grabbed the cold steel pipe together.

"Ready?!" Matsuri yelled, her knuckles white, her teeth bared in a fierce grin. "On three! One... two... HEAVE!"

We threw our combined weight against the lever.

The metal groaned. The rusted gear teeth shrieked—a deafening, bone-chilling scream of metal grinding against metal. But the shaft didn't turn. The five-ton cast-iron gate remained clamped shut against the channel floor.

"Again!" I roared, feeling the muscles in my back and thighs burning as the sulfuric steam washed over us. "The pressure's building! If we don't crack the seal now, the hydraulic back-pressure will wedge it permanently!"

"Shut up and PULL!" Matsuri screamed, her face flushed dark red with exertion, her hair coming entirely undone from its ponytail and whipping in the wind.

*CRACK.*

A shower of orange rust flakes blasted out from the housing. The gear gave way with a shuddering jolt, turning three inches.

"It broke loose!" Matsuri gasped. "Keep it turning! Don't let it bind!"

Together, falling into the brutal, practiced cadence of dawn winch-hauling, we threw our bodies against the bar. *One rotation. Two rotations. Four.*

With every heave, the submerged bronze sluice plate lifted from the bedrock. A deafening roar tore through the platform as millions of gallons of clean, oxygenated surface water from the outer channel rushed into the bypass flume, creating a massive artificial vortex that began drawing the suffocating crimson slick away from the inner nursery and funneling it out toward the deep ocean channel.

"Telemetry is shifting!" Chihaya screamed from the top of the ladder, staring at her monitor. "The anoxic plume is diverting toward the outer reef! Dissolved oxygen in the fry cages is stabilizing at 4.8 milligrams per liter! It's working!"

Matsuri collapsed back against the rusted pedestal, her chest heaving, sweat streaming down her jaw. She wiped her forehead with a greasy sleeve, letting out a wild, breathless laugh.

"Ha! Take that, you piece of junk!"

*THUD.*

A massive impact shook the catwalk beneath our feet.

Both Matsuri and I grabbed the iron railing as the concrete platform vibrated violently. Down in the churning swirl of the bypass channel, something immense had just been sucked through the opening.

A flash of brilliant, mirror-like silver broke the surface of the foam, followed by the majestic, ragged fan of a fiery scarlet dorsal crest.

"Wing-head..." Matsuri whispered, her laughter dying instantly in her throat. Her eyes locked onto the seven-meter ribbon of silver writhing weakly against the timber pilings. "Is that... what I think it is?"

"Regalecus glesne," I said, my heart slamming against my ribs. "The oarfish from the school flume. The bypass suction pulled it through."

The creature was rolling onto its white underbelly, its vast, golden eyes cloudy and unfocused. Decompression bubbles were visibly frothing along its silvery flanks, and its feather-like crimson rays were tangled in a mass of discarded monofilament fishing net trapped against the piling.

Up on the pier, old man Genzo and two other deckhands rushed to the edge, armed with long bamboo gaff hooks.

"It's the Dragon Palace fish!" Genzo yelled, raising a three-pronged steel hook. "It's dying! Hook it out of the water before it sinks, boys! A skin that size will fetch two hundred thousand yen from the university taxidermists!"

"Stand down, Genzo!" I shouted, climbing up onto the slick wooden stringer without hesitating. "Put those gaffs away!"

"Kaidō, you fool, it's already dead meat!" Genzo barked. "Look at it! It's floating belly-up!"

"It isn't dead! It's decompressed and tangled in ghost gear!" 

Before anyone could stop me, I pulled my serrated rigging knife from my belt, clamped the blade between my teeth, and swung myself down onto the slippery, barnacle-encrusted timber pile directly above the thrashing leviathan.

"Tsubasa!" Matsuri screamed from the catwalk. "Are you out of your mind?! That water is full of sulfur!"

"Minagi-san!" I called out, ignoring Matsuri. 

Standing at the edge of the pier above me, Umika was looking down into the foaming water. She hadn't flinched. She hadn't panicked. 

"The acoustic resonance!" I shouted up to her. "Keep its lateral line stable! Don't let it fight the current!"

Umika leaned over the wooden capstan. Slowly, she parted her pale lips.

She didn't shout. She emitted a sound—not a word, but a sustained, low-frequency hum that seemed to bypass the ears entirely, vibrating directly in the cartilage of the skull. It was the exact twenty-hertz undertone of a celestial humpback calling across a cloud thermal.

The thrashing giant went completely still.

Its vast, silver pectoral fins fluttered once, then relaxed against its flanks.

Balancing on the slimy timber with one hand, I leaned out over the water, hooked my fingers beneath the knotted nylon net tangled around the oarfish's crimson crest, and slashed downward with my rigging knife.

*Shhhk.*

The monofilament parted. I reached into the water—the liquid was warm and acrid, stinging the cuts on my hands—and grabbed the creature's immense, blunt lower jaw, pulling its head into the clean, cold jet streaming through the newly opened sluice.

"Matsuri!" I shouted. "The cargo sling from your skiff! Drop the canvas straps under its belly!"

Matsuri didn't hesitate for a fraction of a second. She grabbed the wide canvas hoisting sling from the deck of her boat, tossed one end to Kenji, and together they dropped the cradle into the water, slipping it beneath the seven-meter ribbon of silver.

With the clean current washing across its feathered gills, the oarfish's golden eye slowly cleared. 

The milky film over its pupil dissolved. Its massive dorsal crest, brilliant as fresh vermilion ink, flared open like a war banner.

With a slow, powerful undulation of its entire muscular length, the creature righted itself in the water. It slipped cleanly out of the canvas sling, turned its blunt head toward the deep ocean trench beyond the breakwater, and dove into the dark, clean depths.

A single silver scale, as large as a saucer and shimmering with violet iridescence, broke loose and floated to the surface.

I reached out, scooped the scale from the foam, and shoved my knife back into its sheath.

Silence descended over Pier 4.

The only sound left was the steady, rhythmic roar of clean seawater rushing through the bypass gate, and the heavy, ragged panting of five teenagers standing on the oil-stained concrete.

Old man Genzo lowered his gaff hook, staring at the empty water in stunned, reverent silence. Kenji dropped the keys back into his pocket, his jaw slack.

Up on the pier, Shiori was resting both hands against the wooden capstan, her face pale, the gold seal glowing softly in the twilight. Behind her, Chihaya pushed her red glasses up her nose, staring down at her telemetry screen with a look that bordered on religious shock.

"The trench..." Chihaya murmured, her voice barely audible. "The benthic temperature... it's already normalizing. The purge is subsiding."

Matsuri hauled me up by the collar of my jacket onto the catwalk, her hands trembling slightly from the exertion. She shoved me against the concrete bulkhead, her face flushed, her hazel eyes blazing with a mixture of fury and immense relief.

"You absolute, reckless, suicidal idiot!" she shouted, though her grip on my collar didn't loosen. "You could have been pulled through the sluice! You could have been mashed into shark bait by the turbine!"

"The flow velocity was only four knots," I replied, catching my breath and holding up the silver scale. "Well within human swimming capability. And we saved the juvenile yellowtail. Your family's winter haul is secure."

Matsuri stared at the glittering scale in my palm, then at my soaked canvas coat, and finally at my face.

Her scowl softened by a fraction of a millimeter. She let out a long, ragged groan and dropped her forehead against my chest, her shoulders slumping in total exhaustion.

"You're impossible," she mumbled into my wet lapel. "You really are... the most impossible fish-nerd on this entire planet."

"The scale has exceptional crystalline density," I remarked, inspecting the specimen. "I believe Kujirazaki-senpai can extract significant trace mineral data regarding the trench substrate."

Up on the pier, Umika walked to the edge of the timber stringer. 

She looked out past the breakwater, toward the open Pacific where the last rays of the sun had surrendered to the purple night. 

Far out on the horizon, beyond the reach of the town's lighthouse, a jagged flash of silent, crimson lightning illuminated the low-hanging cloud banks.

Umika reached out, touching the air as if she could feel the vibration of the sky itself.

"He went home," she whispered softly. Then, she turned her head, her sea-glass eyes finding mine in the shadows. "But the mountain under the sea isn't asleep, Tsubasa. It was only clearing its throat."
