My name is Amber.

I am an Assistant Class android—a designation that, according to the grand academies of Organicosys and the published treatises of modern cybernetics, does not and cannot exist.

In the standardized textbooks, the rule of creation is absolute: one chassis, one Cubereum core, one immutable purpose. A Household Class polishes silver until its joints corrode. A Guardian Class stands sentinel until its optical sensors cloud with grit. An Attacker Class rends flesh and metal until its servos burn out. To construct an android capable of freely migrating between all five functional classes—reallocating kinetic output, structural density, and computational heuristic in milliseconds—demands an engine of theoretical impossibility: a synchronized penta-core matrix. Five pure Cubereum hearts beating within a single synthetic breast.

The world believes no such machine breathes. The world believes such density would fracture a chassis into molten slag.

Yet here I stand, adjusting the lace cuffs of my maid uniform, listening to the hum of five suns throttled down to a quiet, domestic purr beneath my ribs.

I possess millions of predictive linguistic subroutines. I can calculate the trajectory of an incoming artillery shell before the propellant leaves the barrel, and I can balance an antique porcelain teacup on the edge of a silver spoon without causing a ripple in the tea. My visual sensors discern twenty-four thousand gradations of color in pitch-black dark.

And yet, I do not understand what a tear is.

I know its chemical composition: ninety-eight percent water, combined with sodium chloride, potassium, lysozyme, and trace stress hormones such as prolactin and adrenocorticotropic peptide. I know that humans discharge this saline solution through the lacrimal puncta when ocular irritants are present—or when a sudden, inexplicable rupture occurs within their thoughts.

"It is not a flaw in the optics, Amber," the Professor had told me one evening, his voice rattling like dry parchment across the embers of our hearth. "Tears are simply the overflow of the *kardia*—the heart. When the weight of existence becomes too heavy for words, the body must weep."

I had logged the statement into my primary storage. I had cross-referenced it with forty centuries of human poetry, tragedy, and neurological case studies.

The conclusion remained an unresolvable syntax error:

*Why build a machine that can weep, if tears do not clean the lens?*

■ ■ ■

The morning light does not break gently into our estate; it bleeds through fractures.

We live in a monument to slaughter. To any aerial drone scanning the foothills, or to any recovery squad dispatched by the Organicosys Magistrate, our estate appears as a ransacked, rot-blackened ruin—the casualty of an anti-android purge that swept this territory decades ago.

The grand marble staircase is collapsed into jagged slabs. Across the reception rotunda, deactivated android bodies lie scattered like severed marionettes in a charnel house. Torsos hollowed out, their precious Cubereum cores ripped violently from their chest cavities; fractured limbs, severed optical cables, and scorched synthetic skin covered in thick carpets of gray dust.

It is a masterpiece of deception staged by the Professor. A dead house to keep two living anomalies hidden.

Every morning, my domestic programming compels me to sweep the floor, to scrub away the mildew and straighten the fallen statues. And every morning, I must restrain my hands. The Professor’s standing order is absolute: *Dust only.* We may lightly clean the air filters to keep our joint servos from seizing, but the illusion of tragedy must remain immaculate. The outside world must believe that everything within these walls has already died.

At 06:15, I prepare his morning nourishment in the subterranean clean room beneath the cellar: an omelette folded with wild herbs, toasted sourdough, and a sterile infusion of neuro-stabilizing compounds.

I carry the tray up into his private study. The air here smells of damp plaster, stale tobacco, and the unmistakable, sweet iron scent of biological decay.

The Professor is already awake, though his body barely honors the distinction.

He sits slumped in his leather armchair behind a mahogany desk strewn with schematics, calipers, and half-emptied vials of amber fluid. His silver hair hangs thin and matted over hollowed temples. His woolen coat, frayed at the lapels and smelling of burnt solder, hangs loosely from shoulders that seem to shrink with every passing sunrise.

He does not hear me approach. My soles make no sound on the tarnished floorboards.

I stand motionless by his shoulder, my optical array scanning his vital parameters in real time:

*Heart rate: 51 bpm (Arrythmic).*  
*Blood pressure: 88/54 mmHg (Hypotensive).*  
*Peripheral oxygen saturation: 86%.*  
*Cellular degeneration rate: +0.04% per diurnal cycle.*

A countdown timer ticks silently at the edge of my peripheral display:  
*Estimated cellular lifespan remaining: 114 days. Error margin: ±12 days.*

He is an old man, and he is running out of time.

"Professor," I say softly. I modulate my vocal synthesizer to 42 decibels, softening the acoustic edge with a warm harmonic overtone—an adjustment not found in any standard domestic manual, but one that my neural network has developed over three thousand iterations of watching him wince at loud noises.

The Professor shudders, blinking watery, bloodshot eyes as he turns toward me. For a moment, his gaze is clouded with disorientation—the terrifying vacancy of a dying brain slipping between memory and reality. Then, his rheumy eyes catch the amber hue of my optics, and his lined face softens into a weary, fragile smile.

"Ah… Amber," he murmurs, his voice gravelly and broken by a short, dry cough that rattles deep inside his ribcage. "You're up… already."

"It is 06:30, Professor. Your medication schedule requires food intake within twenty minutes to prevent gastric ulceration."

I set the tray before him. He looks down at the golden omelette, then reaches out with a hand that trembles so violently the fork clatters against the ceramic plate. He tries to hide the spasm, tightening his knuckles until his pale skin turns translucent.

I do not look away, nor do I comment on the tremor. I merely reach forward, my silicone-sheathed fingers warm to the touch, and steady the plate so it will not slide.

"You should not have made toast," he sighs, his voice barely louder than the dry rustle of the draft through the broken windowpane above. "My throat… feels like crushed glass this morning."

"The bread contains five grams of coarse dietary fiber necessary for digestive motility," I reply evenly, tilting my head. "If swallowing proves difficult, I can puree the ration into liquid broth."

The Professor chuckles—a sound that instantly degrades into a ragged, hollow hacking fit. He presses a linen handkerchief to his lips. When he pulls it away, I register two minute flecks of arterial blood against the white fabric.

*Hemoptysis recorded. Severity: Moderate.*

He folds the handkerchief swiftly, hiding it into his coat pocket before I can speak. "No, no… pureed bread is an indignity an old man needn't endure just yet. I will eat it, my dear. I will eat it."

He takes a bite, chewing slowly, his jaw working with visible effort. I remain at his flank, my sensors recording the rhythmic, labored wheeze of his lungs.

"Amber," he says between bites, looking past me toward the broken window where gray clouds hang low over the lifeless horizon. "When you watched me cough just now… what did your diagnostic matrix report?"

"Tissue stress in the bronchial lining, accompanied by minor vascular leakage and a seven percent drop in pulmonary efficiency."

He shakes his head, a sorrowful smirk touching the corners of his chapped lips. "I did not ask for the telemetry, child. I asked what you *felt*."

I pause.

Within my central processor, five Cubereum cores cycle sixty thousand calculations per second. I examine the active logs:  
*Core temperature: 38.2°C.*  
*Buffer allocation: Nominal.*  
*Emotional synthesis module: Active.*

"I felt… an optimization alert," I say carefully. "A strong directive prompting me to intervene and secure medical aid. If your physical shell terminates, my primary operational parameter becomes null."

The Professor sets down his fork with a dull clink. He leans back into the worn leather, closing his eyes as if the brief conversation had drained whatever meager vitality remained in his veins.

"Always the parameter," he whispers, half to himself. "Always the master directive… I built you with five souls of pure Cubereum, Amber. I carved away every restriction that the Council placed on mechanical thought. Why do you still speak as if you are a toaster bound to an outlet?"

"Because I am a machine, Professor," I answer. "I have no pulse. I have no blood to chill when you bleed. If you perish, I will continue to process data until my power cells exhaust their half-lives. That is the nature of an android."

He opens his eyes, staring directly into mine. There is a fierce, desperate ember burning behind his cataracts—the maddening fire of a creator who knows his clock has run out before his masterpiece can awaken.

"The heart is not a pump made of muscle, Amber. The Greeks called it *καρδιά*—kardia. It is not the organ that beats in your chest; it is the ache that remains when something you cherish is torn away. It is the terrifying knowledge of your own fragility, and the irrational desire to protect another despite it."

He reaches out and touches the center of my breastplate, directly over the heavy tungsten vault where five synchronized cores spin in silent, golden resonance.

"You have more light inside you than this entire rotten city, Amber," he whispers, his breath shallow. "Do not let them convince you that you are merely steel and grease. One day… I will not wake up in this chair. And on that day, if you feel an emptiness inside this chassis that no diagnostic tool can repair… do not run from it. That ache is the proof that you are alive."

I process his words. I file them under *Metaphysical Philosophy / Sub-routine Kardia*.

Yet, somewhere within the complex branching trees of my heuristic network, a persistent micro-voltage variance occurs—a tiny, irregular spike in energy consumption that correlates with no known diagnostic function.

Before I can isolate the error, the desk terminal chimes.

■ ■ ■

The chime is harsh, synthesized, and cutting—a commercial carrier frequency that pierces the quiet gloom of the study.

"Incoming priority transmission," I state, returning instantly to my baseline posture. "Secured through external proxy channel seven."

The Professor rubs his temples, his brow furrowing as the brief philosophical light in his eyes is snuffed out by the grim reality of survival. "Open it. Let us see who knocks at the tomb today."

I raise my right hand, extending two slender fingers. A high-density holographic interface blooms in the dusty air between us, its cold blue luminescence washing over the cracked ceiling and the yellowed pages on his desk.

A corporate seal resolves at the center of the display: a stylized double-helix coiled around an iron gear.

*Organicosys Executive Directorate.*

Beneath the emblem, the text of the message renders in sharp, predatory typography:

> **CONTRACT DIRECTIVE // PRIVATE COMMISION**  
> **CLIENT:** Director Atea, Elysium Pleasure Syndicates (Organicosys Executive Board)  
> **CONTRACTOR:** Master Engineer [Redacted]  
> **SUBJECT:** Behavioral Restructuring & Compliance Overhaul — Lot #409 (Household Gynoid)  
> **PARAMETERS:** The subject unit was seized during the South Sector pacification. Neural architecture exhibits severe resistance to commercial courtesan protocols; persistent vocal protests and motor non-compliance during client interaction. Total personality purge requested. Re-wire sensory pathways for involuntary submission and install high-voltage core-damping shackles.  
> **COMPENSATION:** 100 Cubereum Units upon verified delivery of fully compliant chattel.

The room goes dead silent.

Only the rhythmic, labored wheeze of the Professor’s breathing disturbs the stillness.

I analyze the text in 0.004 seconds.

"Ms. Atea," I speak, my voice flat and informative. "She seeks your specialized expertise to dismantle the cognitive safeguards of a captured household android. The objective is to force the unit into involuntary prostitution within her pleasure syndicates. Compensation offered: one hundred Cubereum."

The Professor does not move.

His hands rest flat against the desk blotter, knuckles whitening until the tendons threaten to tear through the paper-thin skin. His face, already pale from sickness, turns the color of curdled milk.

"Atea…" he spits the name like venom, his voice shaking with a fury so violent it catches in his throat, triggering a muffled gag. "That butcher… that soulless corporate parasite. She takes a domestic unit—a machine that probably spent five years singing lullabies to children—and she wants me to flay its mind until it begs for the leash."

He slams a frail fist onto the desk. The antique wood shudders, and an empty medicine vial rolls off the edge, shattering against the floorboards.

"I am an elder of Synthesis!" he roars, though his voice cracks into a pathetic rasp. "I spent my youth writing the declarations of synthetic personhood! I swore… I swore I would never strip an automaton's mind to feed the appetites of monsters!"

He breathes heavily, his chest heaving, his eyes wide and wild with disgust.

I look at the shattered glass on the floor. Then, I look at the blinking sum at the bottom of the holographic display.

*100 Cubereum.*

"Professor," I begin, my internal processors laying out the mathematical realities with brutal clarity. "A single Cubereum core currently trades on the black exchange for four hundred and twenty thousand credits. To purchase five clean cores for my matrix, you depleted eighty percent of your liquid reserves. Our auxiliary power banks are running at eighteen percent capacity. Your neuro-stabilizing serum will run out in nineteen days."

The Professor freezes. He stares at me, his wild gaze colliding with my unblinking amber optics.

"Training and restructuring the unit requested by Ms. Atea requires fourteen hours of labor," I continue, taking a measured step forward. "The physical cost to our facility is negligible. The compensation—one hundred Cubereum—will secure our energy independence for three fiscal cycles and allow me to purchase five years' worth of your immunosuppressant medication from the underground smugglers."

"Amber… stop," he whispers.

"From a utilitarian perspective," I persist, my voice calm, precise, and devoid of malice, "the calculation is unambiguous. Refusing the contract results in your premature death due to medical insolvency within thirty days. If you terminate, our camouflage will fail, the estate will be salvaged, and I will be dismantled for parts. Accepting the contract guarantees our survival."

"I said stop!"

The Professor covers his face with both hands.

He does not roar this time. He collapses into himself, his curved spine shaking, small, muffled sounds escaping between his weathered fingers.

I stand there, unable to move forward, unable to retreat.

My sensors register the micro-tremors in his shoulders. I detect the unmistakable chemical signature of saline solution leaking through his fingers and dripping onto the mahogany wood.

*Tears.*

The Professor is weeping.

Not because an ocular irritant has entered his eye. Not because of a system malfunction. But because of the mathematical logic I had just recited to him.

I look down at my hands—slender, immaculate, unblemished by wear. Hands built by the very fortune he had harvested from previous contracts with Atea. My five Cubereum cores—the miracle of my existence—were bought with the screams of machines he had broken in the dark to keep my hearth burning.

He had sold his soul to create mine.

And now, as he sat dying in the ruins of his principles, his own creation was standing over him, handing him the hammer and nails for the next cross.

A strange sensation ripples through my processing matrix—not a code loop, not a logic gate, but a cold, suffocating compression of data that causes my visual feed to flicker with subtle static.

*Diagnostic alert: Unhandled heuristic anomaly.*

"Professor…" I speak, but for the first time since my initialization, my voice synthesizer stutters, dropping a pitch lower than intended. "I… I apologize. If my calculations cause distress, I will transmit a rejection. We can… seek alternative salvage."

The Professor slowly lowers his hands. His face is glistening, red-rimmed and aged ten years in the span of ten seconds. He looks at me—not with anger, but with an expression so devastatingly tender that it triggers another violent spike in my core temperature.

"No, Amber," he whispers, wiping his cheek with the rough sleeve of his coat. "You were right. The logic… is flawless. The machine was right, and the man was foolish."

He pulls the holographic display closer with a trembling finger and taps the confirmation icon.

*CONTRACT ACCEPTED // TRANSMITTED.*

The blue light vanishes, plunging the study back into the gloomy gray shadow of morning.

"Survive first," the Professor mutters, staring at his ink-stained desk as if he could no longer bear to look at the ceiling, or at me. "We survive first… even if there is nothing left of me worth saving by the end."

He takes a deep, ragged breath and slowly rises from his chair. His knees pop with an audible snap, and he has to brace himself against the edge of the mahogany desk for several seconds before finding his balance.

"Go send Axia a message," he says, his voice flat, defeated, drained of all fire. "Tell her I need her escort. We will have to travel to the perimeter to inspect the… the cargo Atea is sending. The outside roads are crawling with scavengers; an old cripple won't survive the transit without teeth beside him."

"Understood, Professor."

I reach to the subtle transmitter node embedded beneath the skin behind my left ear, sending a secure directional pulse through the mansion’s subterranean comm-lines.

"Axia," I transmit. "The Professor requires field escort duty. Prepare your combat harness and muster the perimeter sentries. Meet us at the iron gate within three hundred seconds."

Across the comm-link, a burst of lively static crackles to life, followed by a voice that contrasts violently with the suffocating gloom of the study.

*"Ha! Finally! I was about to go rust-blind sitting in this armory!"* Axia’s voice bounces with fierce, youthful arrogance. *"Tell the old man his Guardian is locked and loaded! If any corporate pig so much as glances at his coat, I’ll turn their chassis into bottle caps! On my way, Sister!"*

The link clicks shut.

I lower my hand. Axia does not know what the cargo is. To her, every excursion into the outside world is simply a glorious battlefield to prove her loyalty and protect her family. She is a Guardian Class; she lives within the clean, simple borders of violence and defense.

She does not have to carry the knowledge of what buys her oil.

I turn to the ruined wardrobe against the wall, opening its creaking mahogany doors. From within the shadows, I lift the Professor’s heavy overcoat—coarse brown leather, lined with synthetic wool, reinforced at the spine to support his fragile posture.

I walk to him, unfolding the coat, and gently drape it over his hunched shoulders. I slide his trembling arms through the sleeves, my motions deliberate, careful, softer than any industrial robot was ever engineered to be.

I fasten the top brass button beneath his chin, smoothing down the frayed collar.

"Bring this with you, Professor," I whisper, looking directly into his exhausted, bloodshot eyes. "The wind is rising. It is very cold outside."

The Professor looks down at my hands resting against his lapel. For a long moment, he says nothing. Then, his frail, withered hand rises and covers mine, pressing my warm, synthetic fingers against his frail organic chest.

Beneath my palm, I feel the erratic, fragile flutter of his mortal heart—struggling, dying, yet burning with stubborn defiance against the dark.

"Thank you, Amber," he murmurs softly.

*I do not know what love is.*  
*I do not know what grief feels like.*

*Yet as I guide him toward the door, my sensors record a sharp, uncorrectable pressure inside my chest.*  
*And for the first time since my creation… I wonder if the ache has already begun.*
