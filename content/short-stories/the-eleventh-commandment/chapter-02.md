# The Vision in the Basilica

The stone floor of the basilica of Ravenna was never truly warm, but in the eleventh month, it bit through wool like frost.

I, Aureus Saturnus Taxatio, priest of the holy see and administrator of the bishop’s granaries, was alone.

The last of the altar candles had guttered out an hour past midnight, leaving behind the bitter perfume of melted tallow, stale incense, and the damp salt air that constantly drifted inward from the Adriatic marshes. Before me lay the altar of cedar and marble; behind me, thirty rows of empty wooden benches stretched into darkness like the ribcage of a beached leviathan.

My knees were stiff. My fingers, stained blue and black from twenty years of iron gall ink and tally sticks, could barely feel the wooden beads of my rosary. 

I had spent eighteen hours auditing the diocesan accounts. Three barrels of sacramental wine had vanished from the cellar in Ariminum. Two local deacons had underreported their barley yields by eleven bushels. And Bishop Ambrose’s courier was due at dawn, expecting the parchment sealed and the deficits explained.

I bowed my head against the cold stone railing, closing my eyes in search of quiet.

"Lord," I whispered into the freezing void of the nave, "grant thy servant peace, or at least a clerk who understands division."

Yet peace did not come, for my mind was still haunted by the stranger from evening vespers.

He had approached me at dusk, a humble tanner from the harbor district whose leather apron smelled of brine and lye. He had lingered until the rest of the congregation departed, twisting his wool cap in calloused, stained hands before the icon of the Savior.

*"Father,"* the man had whispered, his eyes filled with a desperate, timid hunger. *"Is there truly no way to purchase salvation? Can a man not simply pay a fee unto the Almighty and be assured of his soul’s rest?"*

I had smiled then, with all the gentle, condescending patience of an educated priest reciting the catechism. 

*"Peace be upon thee, my son,"* I had replied, placing a hand upon his trembling shoulder. *"Paying of coin is the way of Roman tax collectors and pagan soothsayers, not the way of the Living God. Salvation is no merchandise in a port market. It cannot be bought with gold or silver. It is by our deeds, our repentance, and the unfathomable grace of Christ that we are brought home unto the Father."*

The tanner had nodded solemnly, kissed my ring, and vanished into the fog of the piazza. I had given him the true doctrine. The orthodox doctrine. The holy answer sanctioned by every council from Nicaea to Antioch.

Yet when I retired to my small cell behind the apse—a narrow room packed floor to ceiling with papyrus rolls, bronze balance scales, and grain tallies—the answer felt curiously hollow.

I sat at my trestle desk, staring at the little clay oil lamp as its wick sputtered against the dark.

*Deeds,* I thought, tapping my ink-stained stylus against my thumb. 

Deeds were horribly ambiguous. A man might shelter a pilgrim out of genuine charity, or out of pride, or because the pilgrim promised him a mule in spring. A wicked magistrate might build a chapel with extorted coin and fancy himself washed clean. Even the holy hermits in the desert argued over whether weeping for three hours surpassed fasting for four. Deeds could be forged in the heart. Deeds were subjective, slippery, and plagued by self-deceit.

*A ledger, however, never lies.*

The thought struck me like an illicit thrill, and I quickly crossed myself in the cold dark. 

Yet my mind continued to wander, drifting into forbidden arithmetic. When Caesar demands his due, there is no guessing; the denarius is weighed, the tariff is recorded, the receipt is stamped, and both emperor and citizen part in clear conscience. The transaction is finished. Peace reigns. 

Why, then, should the kingdom of heaven be run like a chaotic bazaar of unmeasured virtues, where no soul ever truly knoweth whether his balance sheet is in the black or drowning in red?

"Forgive me, Lord," I muttered aloud, shivering in the gloom of my cell. "It is late, and my wits are drowned in wine-audits."

I rose, blew out the desk lamp, and stepped back into the freezing nave of the basilica to offer one last penance before sleep.

And that was when the oil lamps flickered.

A sound stirred high in the vaulted rafters—not the scurrying of rats, nor the autumn gale rattling the leaded windowpanes, but the dry, sharp rustling of ten thousand scrolls unrolling at once.
