# The Eleventh Commandment

October 2026 was having an identity crisis, and so was St. Jude’s parish hall.

Outside, rain dripped off the stone gargoyles and splashed against parked hybrid crossovers. Inside, behind double oak doors normally reserved for choir practice and fold-out pancake breakfasts, an improvised tribunal had convened. 

It was called a "court" only because calling it *an uncomfortable argument between five men with lower back pain* sounded undignified. There were no gavels. There was no bailiff. Only a ring of mahogany chairs pulled from the rectory, an electric kettle quietly wheezing in the corner, and the sharp fluorescent flicker of modern LED tubes buzzing above ancient arched stonework.

The subject of today’s closed-door interrogation was the Aureans.

For nearly two years, rumors had circulated through diocesan email threads about this fringe group—quiet, stubborn folk who claimed their canon descended directly from the obscure fourth-century Roman priest, Aureus Saturnus Taxatio. Most respectable scholars regarded Taxatio as either an overzealous tax collector turned monastic satirist, or a sleep-deprived copyist whose footnotes had mutated into dogma. 

The diocesan delegates sat on the left, arms crossed, checking their smartwatches.

Across the low conference table sat the Aurean contingent. They looked neither fanatical nor cowed; if anything, they resembled senior accountants awaiting an audit they knew they were going to win.

After twenty minutes of polite throat-clearing and circuitous theological sparring about apostolic succession, Father Daniel pinched the bridge of his nose. 

"We are not here to excommunicate anyone before lunch," Daniel sighed, waving a hand at the tape recorder on the table. "We just want to know what scripture, precisely, gives your flock the authority to run liturgical services under Taxatio's name."

Silence settled over the room, save for the kettle giving one final hiss.

At the far end of the circle, the representative for the Aureans slowly stood up. 

He didn't speak. Instead, he reached beneath his chair with both hands, groaning slightly as his knees popped, and hoisted a leather-bound volume onto the table. 

It landed with a dull, concussive *thud* that rattled the porcelain teacups. 

The book was monstrous. It wasn't merely a bible; it looked like three family bibles compressed under hydraulic force, bound in cracked calfskin, edged with tarnished brass corners, and so unnervingly thick that it seemed physically incapable of closing flat.

"This," the man announced, resting one broad palm atop the weathered leather with grave solemnity, "is the Book of Aureus the Prophet."

Father Daniel stared at the slab. Monsignor Vance, sitting beside him, adjusted his wire-rimmed glasses, squinting as if assessing the table's structural load capacity. Across the row, a younger parish deacon leaned forward, his mouth slightly agape.

"Aureus... the prophet?" Daniel asked cautiously. "Taxatio was a fourth-century diocesan administrator in Ravenna. He counted grain tithes and certified olive oil shipments. When did he become a prophet?"

"When the mandate was revealed unto him," the man answered without a flicker of doubt.

"Be that as it may," Vance said, gesturing vaguely at the sheer vertical mass between them, "why on earth is it so thick? What are there, four thousand pages in there? The complete *Summa Theologiae* doesn't take up that much airspace. Did your prophet never consider an editor?"

A quiet murmur broke out along the diocesan bench. Someone near the radiator whispered that you could use the tome as a wheel chock for a tractor.

The Aurean representative didn't waver. He simply clasped his hands behind his back and looked Vance dead in the eye with unflinching serenity.

"The thickness of the book does not matter," he said evenly. "What matters is what is inside it."

Daniel rubbed his temples. "Fine. Humour me. How many epistles, testaments, and commentaries are packed into that boulder?"

"None," the Aurean said.

"None?"

"It has three chapters."

A dead silence dropped across the room. Daniel froze. Monsignor Vance blinked once, slowly, took off his glasses, polished them on his cassock, and put them back on.

"I'm sorry," Vance said, leaning forward. "Did you say three *hundred* chapters?"

"Three chapters," the man repeated placidly. "Chapter One: *The Reckoning of the Denarius*. Chapter Two: *The Ledger of Eternal Debits*. And Chapter Three: *The Decree of the Eleventh Stone*."

"Three chapters?!" the young deacon blurted, unable to contain himself. "That book is eight inches tall! What is on those pages? Are the fonts seventy-two points?! Is each sentence illuminated with two pounds of gold leaf?!"

"The margins are wide," the Aurean admitted without embarrassment. "For spiritual annotations. And compound interest tables."

Daniel let out a breath that sounded like a tire deflating. "And what, pray tell, is the divine revelation that warrants three chapters of leather and brass?"

The Aurean bowed his head slightly, placing both hands squarely on the cover as if preparing to invoke the highest mystery of the cosmos.

"We believe," he said, his voice dropping into a solemn, liturgical cadence, "that upon the heights of Mount Sinai, when the dust settled and the tablets were carved, the Almighty was not finished. The ten commandments were merely the preamble. The Lord canonized an eleventh commandment, revealed unto Prophet Taxatio in the year of our Lord 384."

"And that commandment is?" Daniel asked, dreading the answer.

The Aurean looked up, his eyes bright with unshakeable faith.

*"Thy shalt pay."*

A beat of stunned silence passed, followed by an involuntary snort from the corner. 

Then the diocesan bench broke into outright laughter.

Monsignor Vance took off his glasses again, chuckling so hard his shoulders shook. The young deacon buried his face in his hands, trembling with suppressed giggles, while Father Daniel leaned back in his mahogany chair, grinning in sheer disbelief.

"Thy shalt pay?!" Daniel laughed. "Good heavens, man! You're describing a theological toll booth! What is this, the gospel of naked greed?"

"It sounds like a subscription model for paradise," Vance added, wiping a tear of merriment from the corner of his eye. "Why would the Creator of the cosmos command humanity to pay for salvation? Does the Almighty have utility bills? Are the cherubim unionized and demanding cost-of-living adjustments? Does heaven have quarterly overhead?"

"Salvation is grace!" Daniel said, his voice rising above the chuckles echoing off the stone walls. "It is freely given. It cannot be bought, bartered, or financed at prime plus two percent! To say God demands payment is not merely heretical—it is cartoonishly mercenary!"

The laughter rolled across the table, bouncing off the leaded glass windows and the hum of the electric kettle. 

Through it all, the Aurean did not flinch. 

He did not smile. He did not blush. His dark eyes remained calm, holding the steady, patient gravity of a stone monolith watching raindrops slide down its surface. To him, their mockery was neither offensive nor unexpected; it was merely the predictable noise of men who had spent centuries misreading the ledger.

Without a single word of rebuttal, he reached toward the book's flank.

His fingers found the heavy brass clasps. 

*Clack. Clack.*

The metal latches sprang loose with the resonant, mechanical snap of a bank vault unlocking. 

Slowly, with measured reverence, he gripped the corner of the ancient calfskin cover and lifted. A puff of dry cedar and centuries-old parchment drifted into the humid vestry air as the massive board swung open, hitting the tabletop with another soft *thud*.

The laughter on the diocesan side began to wither.

The Aurean turned the heavy, gilded title leaf. Then the frontispiece. 

His hand came to rest at the top of the first page, beneath an ornate, illuminated Latin heading stamped in fading vermilion ink. 

He looked across the table, dead serious, and opened Chapter One.

The diocesan delegates leaned in, expecting verses of poetic devotion, or perhaps apocalyptic prophesies of fire and brimstone. 

Instead, the pages were divided into clean, ruthlessly formatted double columns with tabular margins and indexed sub-clauses. It read less like Genesis and more like an ancient fiscal audit manual drafted by an obsessive-compulsive Roman civil servant.

"Chapter One," the Aurean intoned, his deep voice carrying through the vestry. "*The Reckoning of the Denarius. Section A: Inquiries from Outside the Fold.*"

He ran a broad, calloused finger down a series of numbered subsections.

"Verse fourteen," he recited. "*'If a wanderer from outside the covenant approacheth, possessing curiosity concerning salvation yet desiring neither baptism nor the vows of the faithful, how much shall he remit unto the sanctuary?'*" 

He paused, looking up over the rim of the page.

"Answer: *'Seventy percent of his annual gross harvest, liquid silver, or prevailing local currency.'*"

Monsignor Vance’s jaw twitched. "Seventy percent?! That is not a tithe, that is daylight robbery!"

"Verse fifteen," the Aurean continued in an unflappable drone, entirely indifferent to the interruption. "*'If the same wanderer cometh forth from outside the covenant, yet genuinely desireth to convert and take upon his shoulders the holy yoke of the faithful, how then shall his levy be assessed?'*"

He tapped the table. "Answer: *'Fifty percent, with a grace period of three moons for movable assets and livestock.'*"

"This is sheer lunacy!" Father Daniel exclaimed, throwing his hands into the air. "This is not scripture! It’s the Roman treasury department wrapped in calfskin! Your so-called Aureus wasn't a prophet, he was a disgraced tax clerk with an abacus and delusions of grandeur! A false prophet running a spiritual protection racket!"

"A celestial loan shark," Vance muttered, shaking his head with a bitter smirk. 

Deciding the time had come to dismantle this absurd theology on the spot, Monsignor Vance folded his arms, leaned back in his mahogany chair, and offered a patronizing smile. 

"Very well, my good man," Vance said smoothly. "Let us test the divine omniscience of Prophet Taxatio. Suppose a soul wanders into your sanctuary begging for redemption. She is a widowed mother with seven minor children, one adult son who only finds irregular day-labor, and her sole worldly possession is a sick female donkey with a limp. What does your 'holy prophet' command her to pay for salvation, hmm?"

Vance settled back, exchanging an amused glance with the young deacon. They waited for the man to stammer. They waited for him to admit that no ancient text could possibly account for such a ridiculous, convoluted hypothetical.

The Aurean didn't flinch. 

He didn't blink. 

In fact, the faint shadow of professional satisfaction flickered across his stern face.

Without breaking eye contact, his thick thumbs caught the gilded edge of the massive book. He began flipping pages with clinical, practiced speed—*thwip, thwip, thwip, thwip*—the heavy leaves whistling through the quiet room until he drove his thumb into page 842.

He glanced down, scanned a dense grid of footnotes, and tapped a manicured fingernail against the vellum.

"Verse four hundred and twelve," the Aurean said calmly. "Cross-referenced with Verse four hundred and thirteen, Subsection Nine, Clause C: *'Regarding the Indigent Matron of Seven Offspring and the Eldest of Sporadic Coin.'*"

Father Daniel froze. Vance’s patronizing smirk slowly evaporated.

"'If the mother hath seven mouths below the age of accountability,'" the Aurean read in a steady, liturgical cadence, "'she shall receive a statutory dependent exemption of four percent per child, yielding a twenty-eight percent deduction against her core spiritual assessment. The adult son shall be audited independently under Schedule D, provided he sleepeth not beneath her roof for more than three consecutive fortnights in a fiscal year.'"

The room was so quiet you could hear the rain hitting the stained glass outside.

The Aurean turned the page with a gentle rustle.

"And concerning the beast of burden," he continued, eyes scanning verse four-fourteen. "*'If the female ass suffereth from chronic lameness of the hind leg or ailment of the gut, she shall not be assessed as a taxable asset, but instead yieldeth a medical hardship depreciation write-off of four silver sesterces—provided two verified parish elders inspecteth the beast's gums and certify her unfitness for the plough.'*"

He closed his finger over the margin and looked across the table at Monsignor Vance.

"Net spiritual liability: twelve copper pieces, or three bushels of dry barley payable upon the harvest moon. Installment plans are available at four percent interest."

Father Daniel sat paralyzed, staring at the sheer geometric horror of the footnote. The young deacon had stopped giggling entirely, now looking at the massive volume as if it might calculate his student loans if he made sudden movements.

Monsignor Vance swallowed hard, his face flushed. "And... and what of the wretched?" he demanded, his voice straining. "What of the beggar who truly hath nothing? The leper by the roadside who possesses neither twelve coppers nor a solitary grain of barley? Does your merciless accounting cast him into outer darkness because his pockets are empty?!"

The Aurean gave a soft, pitying sigh, turning three pages forward with a gentle flick of his wrist.

"Verse seven hundred and three," he recited solemnly. "*'Regarding Total Insolvency of the Spirit and the Flesh.'*"

He raised his eyes to meet Vance’s gaze.

*"Thou shalt not fret, for God is just."*

The room fell breathless. 

The Aurean pointed down at the golden calligraphy. "*'If a soul hath neither grain, nor coin, nor skin of oil, nor beast of burden, let him not despair before the gates of glory. For the Lord God is not an unmerciful bailiff. His debt shall be entered into the Ledger of Divine Forbearance, interest shall freeze for seven seasons of jubilee, and he shall fulfill his holy obligation through six days of communal roadwork or clearing brush around the parish well, credited at the divine minimum wage of one denarius per cubit cleared.'*"

He looked up. "Divine bankruptcy protection. Fully discharged upon completion of public service."

Daniel rubbed his face with both hands, groaning into his palms. "It’s an entire legal system. You've codified salvation into an municipal tax code."

Suddenly, Daniel’s head snapped up. His eyes narrowed, striking upon the fatal flaw that broke every religious grift throughout human history.

"Wait a moment," Daniel said, pointing an accusatory finger at the book. "What about Aureus himself? The prophet! The author of this monstrosity! I suppose *he* was exempt, wasn't he? Like every charlatan who ever collected a collection plate! Did Taxatio pay his own holy dues, or did he pocket the seventy-percent wanderer tax and sail off to a villa in Capri?!"

A smug murmur stirred among the diocesan delegates. They leaned forward, certain they had cornered the man at last.

The Aurean’s lips twitched into the faintest hint of a smile. 

He didn't search. He simply flipped straight to the very last parchment of Chapter One, where the red ink turned bold and thick. 

"Verse nine hundred and ninety," the Aurean read, his voice ringing like a bronze bell across the vestry.

"*'I knoweth what thou wantest to ask.'*"

Daniel’s jaw dropped.

"Verse nine hundred and ninety-one," the Aurean continued with relish:

*"‘Verily, verily, yes: Aureus Saturnus Taxatio is paying too.’"*

He traced the parchment down to an official stamped seal. "*'Filed in the fourth year of Emperor Theodosius: thirty-four silver denarii, zero exemptions claimed, paid in full unto the common coffer.'*"

He looked across the table at the dumbfounded priests. 

"The prophet audits himself first."

A vein in Monsignor Vance’s temple began to throb visibly. 

Something had broken inside the room. The dignified ecclesiastical inquiry was gone. In its place stood a battlefield of theological desperation. The diocesan delegates were no longer trying to examine the heresy; they were trying to break the book.

"Fine!" the young deacon cried, his hair slightly disheveled as he leaned halfway across the mahogany table. "What if a man is attacked by Mediterranean pirates on the high seas, but the pirate captain is his estranged brother-in-law, and the only plunder taken was seventeen jars of pickled lamprey that were already eighty percent spoiled?! Is the loss deductible as piracy, fraternal gift, or spoiled inventory?!"

The Aurean didn't even flinch. His left hand flicked a chunk of pages backward.

*Thwip.*

"Verse five hundred and sixty-two," he recited smoothly. "*'Regarding Maritime Piracy Among Kinsfolk.'* If the pirate is related by marriage unto the third degree, the theft shall be classified as an informal family settlement unless blood was drawn upon the poop deck. The lampreys shall be written off at twenty percent salvage value, provided the smell thereof was sworn before a notary under oath."

"What if a man gets struck by lightning twice?!" Father Daniel shouted, his voice cracking. "And on the second strike he believes he has become an olive tree?! Does the fruit that falls from his pockets count as agricultural capital gains or taxable bodily harvest?!"

*Thwip-thwip.*

"Verse six hundred and eighteen," the Aurean said without dropping a beat. "*'Concerning the Deluded Arborist.'* He shall be assessed as an olive tree only if he remaineth rooted in soil for forty consecutive suns. Otherwise, all dropped fruit is treated as personal miscellaneous income subject to a fifteen percent pressing fee."

"What if someone robs the church collection plate," Vance shrieked, slamming both palms down onto the table so hard his tea saucer jumped, "and then deposits the exact same stolen silver into the poor box on his way out the door?! Does he receive a charitable tax receipt for grand larceny?!"

The Aurean turned a single leaf with the surgical precision of an archivist handling papyrus.

"Verse eight hundred and four," he answered, his voice steady as granite. "*'The Parable of the Circuitous Scoundrel.'* He hath committed theft under Subsection A, yet rendered charity under Subsection B. The stolen principal shall be restored with five percent restitution, but he may deduct forty percent of the restitution as a moral reconciliation rebate, provided he confesseth before the sun setteth behind the olive groves."

"What if an unbaptized gladiator fakes his own decapitation with a pig's bladder to dodge Roman inheritance tax?!" Vance roared, his face now the shade of a ripe beetroot. "And then uses the death benefit to buy eighty-four barrels of fermented goose grease, which he then donates to a rival pagan cult under the name of his favorite hunting dog?!"

Silence descended on the room. 

Father Daniel stared at Vance in horror. The young deacon covered his mouth. The electric kettle let out one final, terrified bubble.

The Aurean paused. 

For three agonized seconds, nobody breathed. Vance smiled a manic, trembling smile—he had done it. He had finally invented a scenario so thoroughly deranged that no human mind, let alone a fourth-century Roman accountant, could have anticipated it.

The Aurean looked at Vance. 

Then he reached into the spine of the book, pulled out a second red ribbon bookmark, and flipped directly to page 1,402.

"Verse one thousand, one hundred and eighty-nine," the Aurean said quietly.

Vance’s left eyelid began to twitch uncontrollably.

"Clause D," the man read. "*'Regarding the Swinish Decapitation of the Arena Performer and the Goose Grease Bequest.'*"

Daniel slowly sank out of his chair, sliding under the table until only his forehead was visible above the mahogany edge.

"'If the fighter of beasts doth simulate his own demise with the bladder of swine,'" the Aurean read, each syllable falling like a hammer onto an anvil, "'his estate shall be declared void *ab initio*. The eighty-four barrels of goose grease shall be impounded by the diocese for the lubrication of church bells. And concerning the hound...'" 

He paused, adjusting his cuffs.

"'...the hound shall pay nothing, for beasts are without sin, unless the dog was registered as a managing partner of the mercantile syndicate.'"

A heavy, suffocating silence fell upon the vestry.

Father Daniel slowly pulled himself back up onto his chair, his collar crooked, staring at the leather behemoth as if it were a dormant unexploded shell from the Great War. 

The young deacon sat motionless, staring at the open pages. For the first time all morning, the mocking amusement had entirely drained from his face, replaced by a creeping, mathematical dread. 

"It's... it's all connected," the deacon whispered, his eyes wide behind his bangs. "Every verse... the pirate's lamprey ties into the maritime cargo index from page ninety... and the sick donkey exemption offsets the communal feed subsidy in Leviticus... it’s a closed-loop system."

"Quiet, Peter!" Monsignor Vance snapped, though his own voice lacked its former imperious thunder. 

Vance pushed himself up, planting both palms on the table. He was cornered, and like any seasoned diocesan bureaucrat backed into a theological corner, he pivoted from dogma to textual criticism.

"Complexity does not equate to truth!" Vance said harshly, his index finger trembling as he stabbed it toward the book. "A spider's web is complex, yet it exists merely to ensnare the foolish! An obsession with detail is the hallmark of madness, not divine inspiration! What if this entire manuscript is a fabrication? What if some medieval prior, or some Renaissance swindler, or your beloved Taxatio himself simply sat down with an inkwell and made the numbers up?!"

Vance leaned in, his eyes blazing with desperate triumph. 

"Human error, my dear man! Scriptural corruption! Even the Holy Gospels have textual variants, scribal glosses, and translation discrepancies! How do you know someone didn't inflate the seventy percent to line their own pockets? How do you know the numbers aren't forged?!"

The Aurean sighed. It was the deep, weary sigh of a senior forensic accountant having to explain basic arithmetic to an obstinate junior partner.

"Because it is physically and mathematically impossible to forge," the Aurean said calmly.

"Impossible?!" Vance barked a bitter laugh. "Any scoundrel with a quill can forge a number on parchment!"

"A single number in an ordinary text, yes," the Aurean replied, resting both palms flat upon the open leaves. "In an epistle, a copyist may alter a word, and none shall notice save the philologist. But Taxatio did not write a poem. He wrote a balanced double-entry ledger of the cosmos."

He tapped the left-hand column, then the right.

"Every single figure in Chapter One is tethered to a divine audit trail," the Aurean explained, his voice low and relentless. "The seventy percent non-covenant tariff in verse fourteen directly offsets the municipal grain reserve in verse five hundred. The four-sesterce depreciation on the lame donkey reconciles with the livestock casualty allowances in verse twelve hundred. The interest rates, the dependent deductions, the salvage margins—they are mathematically interdependent."

He looked directly into Vance’s eyes, unblinking.

"If you were to tamper with so much as a single copper coin—if you altered one seventy percent to sixty-nine, or forged a three into a four—the totals at the end of the chapter would fail to reconcile. The divine balance sheet would fracture. You cannot forge one verse of Taxatio, Monsignor. To alter a single number, you would be forced to recalculate, re-index, and rewrite the entire four-thousand-page codex from the opening verse to the final amen."

The Aurean leaned back, his voice dropping into quiet, devastating finality.

"The book verifies itself. It is mathematically immutable."
